# Gonorrhea

*Category: STI / Sexual Health*

## Definition

Gonorrhea (also spelled gonorrhoea) is a sexually transmitted infection caused by the bacterium Neisseria gonorrhoeae. It can infect the urethra, rectum and throat, the eyes, and in women the cervix.

## Transmission

- Through sexual contact with the penis, vagina, mouth or anus of an infected partner.
- Ejaculation does not have to happen for the infection to pass on.
- From a pregnant person to their baby during childbirth.
- It is not spread by casual contact such as hugging or sharing food.
- Having had gonorrhea and been treated does not protect against catching it again.

## Symptoms

When symptoms appear in men, they usually start within about 1 to 14 days of infection:

- Burning or pain when urinating
- White, yellow or green discharge from the penis
- Pain or swelling in one testicle or the scrotum (if the infection reaches the epididymis)
- Rectal infection: discharge, anal itching, soreness, bleeding or painful bowel movements
- Throat infection: sometimes a sore throat, but usually no symptoms
- Eye infection: redness, pain and pus-like discharge

## Asymptomatic infections

- Many men with gonorrhea have no symptoms, and most women have no or very mild symptoms.
- Rectal and throat infections often cause no symptoms.
- People without symptoms can still pass the infection on and can still develop complications.
- This is why testing after a possible exposure matters, even when a person feels well.

## Risk factors

- Any sexually active person can get gonorrhea
- A new partner or more than one partner
- Sex without a condom
- A partner who has gonorrhea or another STI
- A previous gonorrhea or other STI
- Higher reported rates in some groups, including sexually active young people and men who have sex with men (risk depends on behaviours, not identity)

## Prevention

- Abstaining from sex, or being in a mutually monogamous relationship with a partner who has tested negative
- Using condoms correctly and consistently during vaginal, anal and oral sex
- Regular screening if sexually active with new or multiple partners
- Telling recent partners if you are diagnosed, so they can be tested and treated
- Avoiding sex until treatment is complete and any symptoms have gone, as advised by a clinician

## Testing / diagnosis

Testing depends on the type of sex a person has had. In men it is usually a urine sample. Swabs may be taken from the throat or rectum, and from the urethra or cervix in some settings. Laboratory NAAT tests are the most common method, and a culture may be used to check for antibiotic resistance. A test for chlamydia and other STIs (including HIV and syphilis) is usually offered at the same time. A follow-up test may be advised, especially after a throat infection.

## Treatment overview

General information only. Gonorrhea is curable, but treatment must be prescribed by a healthcare professional.

- It is treated with antibiotics. Recommended regimens change over time because some strains of the bacteria have become resistant to common medicines. Drug resistance is a major global concern (WHO).
- Sexual partners should be tested and treated.
- People should avoid sex until treatment is complete and symptoms have settled.
- Anyone whose symptoms continue after treatment should return to a clinician for retesting.
- Self-treating or using leftover antibiotics is not safe and can add to resistance.

## Complications

- Men: painful inflammation of the epididymis, which can affect fertility if untreated; sometimes prostate infection or urethral scarring.
- Women: pelvic inflammatory disease, chronic pelvic pain, ectopic pregnancy and infertility.
- Spread through the blood (disseminated gonococcal infection), causing fever, joint pain or swelling, and skin sores.
- Eye infection that can damage the cornea, including in newborns.
- A higher chance of catching and passing on HIV.

## When to seek medical help

- Discharge from the penis, burning when urinating, or testicle pain
- Sex without a condom with a new partner, or a partner diagnosed with gonorrhea or another STI
- Rectal or throat symptoms after a possible exposure
- Symptoms that continue or return after treatment
- Routine STI screening if you are sexually active with new or multiple partners

## Urgent warning signs (extra, for the website page)

- Fever with joint pain or swelling and a rash or skin sores
- Severe testicle pain or swelling
- Being unable to pass urine
- Painful, red eye with discharge or vision changes

## Myths vs Facts

| Myth | Fact |
| --- | --- |
| You would know if you had gonorrhea. | Many people, especially with throat or rectal infection, have no symptoms but can still pass it on. |
| You can only get it from intercourse with ejaculation. | It spreads through vaginal, anal and oral sex, and ejaculation is not needed. |
| You can catch it from toilet seats, hugging or sharing food. | Gonorrhea is spread through sexual contact and from mother to baby at birth, not through casual contact. |
| Once you have had gonorrhea, you are immune. | Having had it and being treated does not protect you. You can be reinfected. |
| Home remedies or leftover antibiotics will cure it. | It needs testing and a prescribed antibiotic. Wrong or partial treatment leaves the infection and adds to drug resistance. |

## Key Facts

- Gonorrhea is a curable bacterial STI, but antibiotic resistance is a growing global concern.
- Many infections cause no symptoms, so testing is the only way to know.
- It can affect the urethra, rectum, throat and eyes.
- Untreated gonorrhea can cause epididymitis in men and lead to infertility.
- Condoms, regular testing and treating partners help prevent spread. WHO estimates over 1 million new curable STIs occur every day among people aged 15 to 49.
