# Prostatitis

*Category: Prostate Health*

## Definition

Prostatitis is a frequently painful condition that involves inflammation of the prostate and sometimes the areas around it. The prostate is a walnut-shaped gland that is part of the male reproductive system. It sits just below the bladder, surrounds the upper part of the urethra, and makes part of the fluid in semen.

## Types of prostatitis

Health authorities (NIDDK, Mayo Clinic) describe four types:

- Acute bacterial prostatitis: a bacterial infection of the prostate that starts suddenly and can be serious. It is the least common type but the easiest to recognise, because symptoms are sudden and severe.
- Chronic bacterial prostatitis: a bacterial infection of the prostate that develops slowly or keeps coming back. Symptoms are usually milder than in the acute type and may last for months or years.
- Chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS): the most common type. It causes pelvic pain and urinary symptoms that last or come and go, with no evidence of a bacterial infection. Its cause is not fully understood.
- Asymptomatic inflammatory prostatitis: inflammation of the prostate with no symptoms. It is usually found by accident during tests for other problems, does not cause complications and does not need treatment.

## Causes

The cause depends on the type:

- Bacterial types: bacteria travelling from the urethra into the prostate, or infected urine flowing backwards into the prostate ducts.
- Events that let bacteria in: a recent urinary tract infection, a urinary catheter, or a prostate procedure such as a biopsy.
- Other contributing factors: an enlarged prostate, an abnormality of the urinary tract, or injury to the pelvic area or perineum.
- CP/CPPS: the exact cause is unknown. Researchers think it may involve chemicals in the urine, the immune system's response to a past urinary tract infection, or nerve or muscle problems in the pelvis.
- Sexually transmitted infections (for example chlamydia) can be a cause in some men, but prostatitis is not always sexually transmitted.

## Symptoms

Symptoms vary by type and can overlap with other conditions:

- Pain or a burning feeling when passing urine
- Difficulty urinating, such as dribbling, hesitancy or a weak stream
- Needing to urinate often, especially at night, or urgently
- Cloudy urine or blood in the urine
- Pain in the lower abdomen, groin, lower back, or the area between the scrotum and anus (perineum)
- Pain or discomfort in the penis or testicles
- Painful ejaculation
- Fever, chills, muscle aches and flu-like symptoms (mainly with acute bacterial prostatitis)

## Risk factors

- A recent urinary tract infection or bladder infection
- A recent urinary catheter or a prostate biopsy or other urological procedure
- An enlarged prostate or a urinary tract abnormality
- Injury to the pelvic area
- A previous episode of prostatitis
- A weakened immune system, for example with HIV
- Age: prostatitis is the most common urinary tract problem in men under 50, but it can affect men of any age

## Prevention / risk reduction

There is no guaranteed way to prevent prostatitis, but these steps may lower the risk:

- Get urinary symptoms checked early and finish any prescribed treatment for urinary tract infections
- Drink enough fluids and do not hold urine for long periods
- Practise safe sex (condoms, regular testing) to lower the risk of sexually transmitted infections
- Keep the genital area clean
- Tell a healthcare professional about pelvic pain or urinary changes rather than waiting

## Diagnosis

A healthcare professional usually starts with a medical history and physical examination, which may include a digital rectal exam to feel the prostate. Urine tests look for infection. Blood tests, a test of fluid from the prostate, and imaging such as an ultrasound may be used in some cases. Because symptoms can look like those of other conditions, and prostatitis can raise the PSA blood level, clinicians also rule out other problems.

## Treatment overview

General information only. A healthcare professional chooses treatment based on the type and severity.

- Bacterial prostatitis is treated with antibiotics. Acute cases can be severe and sometimes need care in hospital.
- CP/CPPS is managed by relieving symptoms. Options can include medicines that relax the bladder outlet, pain relief, pelvic floor physiotherapy, warm baths and lifestyle changes.
- Asymptomatic inflammatory prostatitis usually needs no treatment.
- People should not start or stop antibiotics without medical advice.

## Complications

- Infection spreading into the blood (sepsis) in severe acute cases
- A collection of pus in the prostate (prostate abscess)
- Sudden inability to pass urine (urinary retention)
- Chronic or recurring pain and repeated infections
- Sexual difficulties, such as painful ejaculation or erectile problems
- Effects on semen quality and fertility in some men

## When to seek medical help

- Pain or burning when urinating that lasts more than a day or two
- Pelvic, groin, lower back or genital pain that does not settle
- Blood in the urine or semen
- Symptoms that return after treatment
- Any urinary symptoms together with a fever

## Urgent warning signs

Get emergency care straight away if any of these occur:

- Being unable to pass urine
- Fever with chills and severe pain in the pelvis, groin or lower back
- Confusion, a very fast heartbeat, or feeling extremely unwell
- Blood in the urine together with fever

## Myths vs Facts

| Myth | Fact |
| --- | --- |
| Prostatitis is always a sexually transmitted infection. | Most cases are not. Bacteria often reach the prostate from the urinary tract, and many cases have no infection at all. STIs are only one possible cause. |
| Prostatitis is prostate cancer, or leads to it. | Prostatitis is inflammation, not cancer, and it is not known to cause cancer. It can raise the PSA blood test, so doctors check for other causes. |
| Only older men get prostatitis. | It is the most common urinary tract problem in men under 50 and the third most common in men over 50 (NIDDK). |
| Every type of prostatitis needs antibiotics. | Only the bacterial types do. CP/CPPS is managed by relieving symptoms, and the asymptomatic type needs no treatment. |
| Once symptoms settle, prostatitis is gone for good. | Chronic forms can come and go for months or years, so follow-up care matters. |

## Key Facts

- There are four types of prostatitis: acute bacterial, chronic bacterial, chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome, and asymptomatic inflammatory.
- Prostatitis is the most common urinary tract problem in men younger than 50.
- Chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome is the most common type, and its exact cause is not known.
- Acute bacterial prostatitis can be serious. Fever with severe pain or being unable to urinate needs urgent care.
- Prostatitis is not cancer, but its symptoms can look like other conditions, so it should be assessed by a professional.
